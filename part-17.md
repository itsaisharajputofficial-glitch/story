# A Mad Man's Fiction: PART SEVENTEEN

*This is a work of fiction of a mad man with mad tastes.*

---

We had been careful. That was the thing I kept telling myself in the days after the photographs. Careful. Every message deleted. Every saree returned to its hook. Every photograph locked away behind a password that lived only in his head and mine. We had been careful, and the carefulness had become a kind of rhythm, a new heartbeat in the house, and I had almost convinced myself that it was enough.

It was not enough. I know that now. It was never going to be enough, because no amount of care can hold back the one thing that care cannot see coming: the ordinary, the accidental, the husband who forgets his glasses on the one afternoon you have stopped expecting him.

But I did not know that yet. I only knew that the house was empty, and the afternoon was ours, and I had let myself, for one hour, forget to be careful at all.

And that was when I heard the door.

The door that opened and closed was the front door.

I heard it from the bed in Deva's room, that particular sound of the old wood and the brass bolt, and I knew, in the same instant, what it meant, and the blood in my veins turned to ice. Ravi was home. Ravi, who should have been at the shop until nine. Ravi, who never, ever, came home in the afternoon.

"Don't move," Deva whispered, and his voice was perfectly calm, and I understood, in that calm, exactly how close we were to the end. He was already moving, already reaching for the camera, already sliding it under the mattress, and I was sitting up, and my heart was a fist, and the whole room, the sarees, the mirror, the bed, all of it, had become a trap with one door.

The sarees were still on the bed. The blue one, the red one, the school saree, laid out like three women who could, any one of them, give us away. The jasmine was on the floor. The camera was in his hand, and then it was under the mattress, and then he was pressing my blouse into my hands, and I was fumbling with the hooks, and the whole room smelled of us, of the shoot and the sweat and the wine-sweet certainty of the last hour, and Ravi's voice was coming up the stairs.

"Hook it," he whispered, and his hands, steady as a surgeon's, did the hooks my shaking fingers could not, and then he was at the door, and his ear was to it, and he was listening, and I was smoothing my hair, and the woman in the mirror, the one I had looked at so tenderly an hour ago, looked back at me now with wild eyes and a flushed face, and I understood, in that second, that she was the evidence. Not the sarees. Not the camera. Her. The look on her face. The glow under her skin. If Ravi saw her, really saw her, he would not need the photographs.

He would see it all, written on her like a confession.

"Priya!" Ravi's voice, from the bottom of the stairs. "Priya, are you home? I forgot my glasses. The accounts are blurring."

I looked at Deva, and Deva looked at me, and for one long, terrible second, neither of us breathed.

And then, without a word, I did the only thing there was to do. I pulled my saree around myself, and I smoothed my hair, and I walked out of his room and into the corridor, and I closed the door behind me, soft as a prayer.

The corridor was the longest I have ever walked. Three doors, and then the stairs, and every step I took was a step away from the truth and toward the man who could not know it, and I kept my face still, and I kept my hands at my sides, and I repeated, in my head, the same word over and over, the way a woman repeats a prayer when she is walking through a fire.

*Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.*

There is a trick to lying to a man who has known you for twenty-five years. You do not look away from him. You do not rush. You do not, above all, let your face show the one thing that will give you away, and the one thing that was burning in me then, hotter than the fear and hotter than the shame: the fact that I had liked it, the nearness, the almost, the single minute on the edge of ruin, and that a part of me, the part I could not reach to silence, was already sorry it was over.

---

"Here," I said, and I held out his glasses, and my hand, thank God, did not shake. "You left them by the telephone."

Ravi took them, and he looked at me, and his eyes, those tired, kind, half-blind eyes, moved over my face, and I saw him notice, for the first time in our whole married life, that I was flushed, that my hair was loose, that my breathing was not quite steady.

"You are unwell?" he said.

"The heat," I said. "I was lying down."

He nodded, slowly, and he put on his glasses, and the world, my world, narrowed to the two of us standing in the hall, and the closed door at the end of the corridor behind me, and the man behind it, holding his breath.

"Where is Deva?" Ravi said, and the words hit me like a slap.

"Deva?" I said, and my voice was so calm I did not recognize it. "At work. Why?"

"I thought I heard," he said, and he looked toward the corridor, toward the closed door at the end of it, and I felt my heart stop, felt the whole world stop, and then he shook his head, and he said, "Nothing. My ears. The accounts have been blurring everything today."

And he turned away, and the corridor behind me was silent as a grave, and behind the closed door, I knew, my son was not breathing.

"I will be late tonight," Ravi said, and he was already turning to go. "The new ledger has errors. Don't wait up."

"Ravi," I said, and I do not know why I said it, only that the guilt, or the terror, or something older than both, pushed the word out of me.

He turned. "What?"

For one second, I stood there, and I thought of all the things I could say. The truth. A confession. A name. And then the second passed, and I said, "Nothing. Go. I will keep your dinner warm."

And he went, and the front door closed, and the sound of it was the most beautiful and the most terrible sound I had ever heard.

For a long time I did not move from the spot where I stood. I could feel the shape of the house around me, the way you feel a held breath. Somewhere outside, Ravi's scooter coughed to life and puttered away, and the sound faded, and the street went quiet, and still I stood there, in the hall, with my back to the closed door, and I thought: *you are still alive. You are still hidden. It is still secret.*

And then, because the relief was too big to hold, I let out a breath I had not known I was holding, and it came out as a sound, half laugh and half sob, and I put my hand over my mouth, and I slid down the wall, and I sat on the floor of my own hallway, and I did not know whether I was laughing or crying or both.

I was both. I was everything. I was a woman who had just been given back her whole life by a pair of forgotten glasses, and the weight of it, and the lightness of it, were more than I knew how to carry.

---

I stood in the hall for a long time after he left, and I did not move, and I did not breathe, and I listened to the silence of the house settle back over us like water closing over a stone.

And then, very quietly, Deva's door opened, and he came out, and he looked at me, and I saw, in his face, the same thing I felt: the sheer, white, shaking aftermath of having been, for one long minute, an inch from the end of everything.

"That was close," he said, and his voice was low and rough.

"Too close," I said.

And then, because I am what I am now, and because the truth of it would not be held down, I said the thing that frightened me most, the thing I had discovered in that frozen minute in the hall, with my husband's eyes on my flushed face and my son hidden behind a door.

"And I liked it."

The words hung in the air between us, and I saw him go very still, and I saw the thing in his eyes change, and I understood, in that moment, that he had seen it too, in me, and that he had not known whether to be afraid of it or to want it.

"Say it again," he said.

"I liked it," I said, and my voice did not shake. "The danger. The almost. The one minute when everything could have ended. I have never felt so alive in my life." I looked at him, and the horror and the hunger were so tangled together that I could not have pulled them apart with a knife. "What is wrong with me, Deva? What have you made me into?"

I wanted him to say it was nothing. That it was the fear, the adrenaline, the near-miss, and that it would pass. And I wanted him, at the same time, to tell me the truth, even if the truth was the thing I already knew and could not say: that the near-miss was not a symptom of what I had become, but the very heart of it, the thing that made the rest of it sing. I wanted to be told I was safe. And I wanted to be told I was ruined. And I could not have said which one I wanted more.

He held me tighter, and he said, against my hair, "I have not made you into anything, maa. I only opened a door. You walked through it." A pause. "And the thing you found on the other side, the liking of the danger, the wanting of the edge, that was not something I put in you. That was already there. It was there the night you first did not pull away from my hand in the kitchen. It was there the first time you said yes and meant it." He pulled back and looked at me. "I did not make you a woman who likes the edge. I only made it safe for you to admit it."

"The photographs," I said, and the word came out before I could stop it. "They are under the mattress. He was in the house, Deva. He was in the hall. If he had gone into your room, if he had sat on the bed, if he had,"

"They are safe," he said, and his hand was on my face, and his voice was steady. "He did not go in. He did not sit. He did not see. And the photographs are going somewhere better than under a mattress." He paused. "There is a locker, at the office. A drawer with a key that only I have. I will take them there tomorrow. They will never come back into this house."

I nodded, and I wanted to believe him, and I did, mostly. But some part of me, the part that had just watched her whole life balance on a pair of forgotten glasses, understood that no locker, no password, no locked drawer, was ever going to make the photographs safe. Because the danger was not in the photographs.

The danger was in us. In the thing we had become. And that thing could not be locked in a drawer.

And then I was crying, and I did not know why, and he was holding me, and I was shaking, and somewhere, in the back of my mind, behind the terror and the thrill and the shaking, a memory was rising up, old and cold, and it had my mother's face.

My body was still humming from the near-miss. That was the part I could not get past. Not the fear, though the fear was real. The humming. The way my skin felt too tight, the way my heart was still going like a drum, the way my cunt, God help me, was still wet from the knowledge of how close we had come. The danger had done to me what no touch had done in weeks, and the shame of that, and the wonder of that, were the same thing, and they were both mine.

*This is what I am now,* I thought, and the thought was not a judgment. It was a fact, cold and bright and undeniable. *A woman who comes alive at the edge of the fall.*

And somewhere under the humming, under the shame and the wonder, the memory of my mother stirred, and it was not a comfort, and it was not a warning.

It was a mirror.

---

I saw her. Meghna. Standing at the door of our old house, in her thin saree, her hair loose, looking down the road for something that never came. And then I saw her on the floor of the temple, prostrating herself so long that the priest had to ask her twice to rise. And then I saw her at the end, the day they took her away, her eyes wild and her mouth open and her hands reaching for me, and I heard her voice, the last thing she ever said to me, and the words were the same words that had been running through my head for weeks.

I remembered, suddenly and completely, the smell of her. Not the temple smell, not the jasmine. The other smell, the one that came off her in the last year before they took her, that sharp, sweet, feverish smell of a woman burning from the inside. She would sit by the window for hours, and she would not eat, and she would not speak, and her eyes, when she looked at me, were not the eyes of a mother. They were the eyes of a woman who was already somewhere else, somewhere I could not follow, and the smell of her, that burning smell, hung in the house for weeks after they took her away.

I had spent my whole life afraid of that smell, afraid of becoming the woman who made it.

And now, standing in my own corridor, I caught the faintest trace of it on my own skin, under the soap and the sweat, and I understood that it had never been the smell of madness.

It had been the smell of want, denied for too long, finally turning to smoke.

*I am burning, Priya. I am burning, and no one will let me burn in peace.*

They never told me, properly, what was wrong with her. In our house, such things were not said. There was only the closed door, and the whispers in the kitchen, and the day the men came and took her away, and the way my father did not meet my eyes for a month. And then there was the silence, the long silence, the one that had lasted my whole life, in which her name was never spoken and her photograph was turned to the wall and I learned, without ever being taught, that a woman who wants too much is a woman who gets taken away.

And now, here I was, forty-four years old, standing in my own corridor with her blood in my veins and her fire in my chest, and I understood that I had spent my whole life running from a story I had never once been allowed to hear the truth of. Maybe she had not gone mad. Maybe she had only burned, and the burning had frightened them, and they had put her out.

And maybe I was not becoming her. Maybe I had always been her, and the only difference now was that I had stopped pretending otherwise.

And I understood, standing in the corridor of my own house, in my son's arms, with my husband's glasses still warm on the hall table where he had left them, that I had become her. Not in the way I had always feared, the madness, the wildness, the being taken away. In the other way. The way of the burning. The way of wanting something so much that the wanting itself became a kind of fire, and the only question, the only question that had ever mattered, was whether you would be allowed to burn in peace, or whether they would come and put you out.

"I am becoming her," I whispered, and I did not know if I was telling him or myself. "My mother. I am becoming my mother."

He held me, and he did not argue, and he did not soothe, and after a long moment, he said, very quietly, "And is that so terrible, maa?"

I pulled back and looked at him, and the tears were still on my face, and I said, "She went mad. They took her away. They said her wanting had broken her."

"They said the same thing about every woman who ever wanted too loudly." His voice was steady, and his eyes were on mine, and there was no wolf in them now, and no boy. Only the man, and the truth. "They said it about the women who burned, and the women who danced, and the women who loved the wrong man, and the women who loved at all. And they were wrong about all of them." He cupped my face, and his thumbs wiped the tears away, gently, the way he had once wiped chalk from a child's cheek. "They were wrong about her, maa. And they would be wrong about you."

"And if they are not?" I said. "If this," I gestured, at the house, at the closed door, at the two of us, "is the madness, and I am only the latest one to catch it?"

"Then it is a madness that has made you more alive than I have ever seen you," he said. "And I would rather have you mad and mine and alive, than sane and hidden and dead, the way you were for twenty-five years." He pressed his forehead to mine. "Tell me I am wrong."

He was honest. That was the thing I had always known about him, even when I did not want to know it. He was honest where I was not. He had never once, from the first night in his room with the blue saree, pretended that what he wanted from me was anything but a want. I was the one who had dressed it up as a cure, as a service, as a mother's duty, as anything but the plain, burning truth of it.

"I never pretended, maa," he said, and his voice was quiet, and it cut through the dark of my own excuses like a blade through water. "Not once. I told you what I wanted, and I let you choose. And you chose. Not because I tricked you. Not because I forced you. Because you wanted to." He looked at me, and his eyes were steady. "So stop pretending you are the victim of this. You are not a victim. You are the author. You have been the author from the first yes."

And he was right. God help me, he was right, and the knowing of it was the most terrible and the most freeing thing I had ever felt.

I had spent twenty-five years being told, in a thousand small ways, that a good woman does not want. And here was the man I had raised, the man I had taught to say please and thank you, telling me the opposite, telling me that the wanting was the whole point, and that I had been its author, and that there was no shame in the authorship, only in the pretending.

And I believed him. That was the worst and the best of it. I believed him.

I could not. That was the thing. I could not tell him he was wrong, because he was not wrong, and because the woman I had been, the sane, hidden, dead woman, was not a woman I could ever go back to being, even if I wanted to.

And I did not want to. That was the darkest part, the part I could only admit in the silence of my own head. I did not want to go back.

"I don't know what to do," I whispered. "I don't know how to be careful and still be this. I don't know how to keep it, and I don't know how to let it go."

"Then don't decide tonight," he said. "Tonight you only rest. And tomorrow, or the day after, when the fear has cooled, you will look at the woman in the mirror, and you will know what she wants. And whatever she wants, maa," he kissed my forehead, "I will be here. Not to take it from you. To give it to you."

And then he led me to my room, and he put me to bed, and he sat with me until I slept, and I dreamed of my mother, and of fire, and of a road that had no end, and I woke in the dark with the taste of ashes in my mouth and the knowledge, cold and certain, that the reckoning I had been running from was not outside me, in the house, in the husband, in the risk of being caught.

It was inside me. And it was time to face it.

There were only three doors in front of me, and I lay in the dark and counted them the way a woman counts her last coins. The first door: stop. Walk away from him, from this, from the fire, and spend the rest of my life as the woman I had been, sane and hidden and cold. The second door: confess. Tell Ravi everything, and let the house fall, and let the world do with us what it would. The third door: choose. Choose him, and the fire, and the danger, and the being seen, and let everything else burn.

And I did not know, lying there, which door I would take. But I knew, with a certainty that frightened me more than any of them, that the woman I had become would not fit through the first door anymore.

She was too big for it. She had grown too large to be hidden.

And whatever I decided, whatever I chose, I could not un-grow her. I could not go back into the small, cold shape I had been pressed into for twenty-five years.

I could only go forward, through one of the two remaining doors, and hope that whatever was on the other side was worth the burning.

Tomorrow. I would decide tomorrow. But tonight, in the dark, with the taste of ashes in my mouth, I already knew which door the woman in the mirror was walking toward.
