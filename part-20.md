# A Mad Man's Fiction: PART TWENTY

*This is a work of fiction of a mad man with mad tastes.*

---

My name is Priya. And if you ask the women in my colony to describe me, they will lower their voices, click their tongues, and call me *sanskari*. Traditional. Proper. The kind of woman who still covers her head when the postman comes, who fasts every Monday, who in twenty-five years of marriage has not given one neighbour one single thing to gossip about.

That is the woman they think they know.

Let me tell you about the woman they do not see.

They do not see the marks under my saree, the small dark bruises at my throat and my hips, left there by a mouth and a pair of hands in the long hours of the night. They do not see the ache between my thighs, the sweet, deep, secret ache of a woman who has been loved until dawn and has risen to make breakfast as if nothing happened. They do not see the way my eyes find his across the table, over the rotis, while my husband reads his paper, and the whole room, to them, is nothing but a family at its breakfast.

They see a sanskari madam, serving her husband, scolding her son to eat properly.

They do not see the woman who, not six hours ago, was bound in silk and blind in the dark and calling out a name that was not her husband's.

And they will never see her. That is the whole, secret, glorious shape of it. They will never see her, and she does not need them to. She has been seen, once and for all, by the only eyes that ever mattered, and that is enough. That is more than enough.

That is everything.

So let me tell you about the body they do not see, the one under the saree, the one that has, in the last few weeks, become more alive than it was in the twenty-five years before.

The breasts are still heavy, and full, and they carry, now, the memory of a mouth that worshiped them in lamplight and steam. The hips are still wide, and they carry the ghost of hands that held them, hard, in the back of a car and against a washroom wall and in a bed where a husband slept. The thighs are still soft, and they carry the ache of a night in the dark, bound and blind and loved past all endurance. And the place between them, the place I could not once even name, carries a heat that no amount of cold water, no amount of prayer, no amount of pretending, has ever been able to put out.

They call me sanskari. And they are right. I still cover my head for the postman, and I still fast on Mondays, and I still, in a thousand small ways, perform the woman they have decided I am.

But under the saree, under the performance, under the sindoor and the jasmine and the four o'clock mornings, there is a woman who has been loved into life, and she is not going back into the dark.

Not for the colony. Not for the world. Not for anyone.

She is here now. And she is staying.

---

The breakfast that morning was the most ordinary and the most extraordinary of my life.

Ravi sat at the head of the table, his glasses on, his paper open, his bad knee propped on the stool, and he ate his rotis and drank his tea and asked me, in that blind, kind way of his, whether I had slept well. And I said yes, I had slept well, and I said it with the evidence of the night still warm on my skin, under my saree, and I did not blush, and I did not falter, and the lie, if it was a lie, did not even feel like one.

I had slept well. I had slept, in the end, in his arms, in the grey light, with the candles burned down and the silk still loose around my wrists, and I had slept better than I had slept in twenty-five years.

Deva came down late, and he sat across from me, and his eyes met mine over his father's shoulder, and there was, in that one look, the whole of the night, and the whole of everything that had come before it, and the whole of whatever was still to come. And no one saw it. No one. Kamala came and went with the tea. Ravi turned his paper. The milkman's bell rang down the street. And the two of us sat at the table like any mother and son, and the secret sat between us, huge and warm and entirely invisible.

"You are quiet this morning," Ravi said to me, and I smiled, and I said, "I am just tired. It is the heat."

And Deva, across the table, hid a smile behind his tea, and I felt the marks on my throat, under the pallu, and I understood that this, this ordinary breakfast, this family at its table, was the final and most perfect act of the whole long play.

The secret held. That was the thing. After everything, after the car and the temple and the cinema and the bed and the hotel and the rooftop and the dark, the secret held, and the world went on being the world, and no one knew, and no one would ever know.

And that was the ending. Not a confession. Not a reckoning. Not a fall from grace in front of the whole colony. Just a breakfast, and a secret, and the two of us, and the long, unbroken road of it stretching out ahead.

It was, I understood, the ending I had wanted all along.

And yet I must tell you the rest of it, the small, ordinary rest, because the small and the ordinary were the whole point.

Kamala came in with the tea, and her eyes went around the table the way they always did, quick and sharp, and I felt, for one cold second, the old fear rise up, and then I let it pass, because there was nothing for her to see. The marks were hidden. The saree was pinned. The woman at the table was the same woman who had been serving breakfast for twenty-five years, and the only difference was inside her, where no one could see, and that was the only place that mattered.

"The milkman has raised his price again," Kamala said, and I said, "Then we will pay it," and the words were so ordinary that they were almost a kind of miracle.

"The food is very good today, maa," Deva said, and the words were the words he had said at a hundred dinners, and they meant something else entirely, and I heard both meanings, the way you hear the bell under the song.

"Eat properly," I said, and my voice was steady, and my face was calm, and under the table, where no one could see, I pressed my knees together and felt the ache of the night, and I did not once let it show.

"You two," Ravi said, not looking up from his paper, "always with the food. Eat, eat, you would think there was nothing else in the world."

And Deva looked at me, over his father's shoulder, and he smiled, that slow, dark, wolf smile, and he said, very quietly, "There is nothing else in the world, papa. Nothing at all."

And only I knew what he meant. And only I ever would.

That evening, Ravi came home with a small box of sweets.

"For you," he said, and he set it on the kitchen counter, and he looked at me, and there was something in his face I had not seen in years, a kind of shy, awkward tenderness, the look of a man who has noticed, dimly, that something has changed in his wife, and does not know what it is, and does not know how to ask.

"You have been," he stopped, and he rubbed the back of his neck, the way he did when he was uncomfortable with his own kindness, "you have been looking well, Priya. Happy. I don't know what it is. But whatever it is, it suits you."

I looked at the sweets, and then at him, and the guilt should have risen up, and the shame, and the terror, and none of them did. There was only a vast, quiet tenderness, and a grief I could not name, and a certainty that I would carry this moment, this small, clumsy, devastating kindness, for the rest of my life.

"Thank you," I said, and I meant it, and I took the sweets, and I did not cry, though my eyes burned.

And that night, when he reached for my hand in the dark, I let him hold it, and I did not pull away, and I did not think of the other hands that had held me, and for one hour, I was a wife again, and it was tender, and it was devastating, and it was, in its own way, the hardest thing I had done in all of this.

Because kindness, it turns out, is harder to betray than trust. Trust is a wall. Kindness is a wound. And the wound, once made, does not close.

I will not pretend that what I did to Ravi was nothing. It was not nothing. It was a wound, and it was real, and it is still there, and it will be there until one of us is gone. And I will carry it, the way I carry everything else, not because I want to, but because it is mine, and because pretending it is not there would be one more lie, and I am done with lies.

But I will say this, and I will mean it. I did not stop loving him. I only stopped being able to live in the cold of his love, the love that fed me and clothed me and never once looked at me, the love that was as much a wall as it was a home. And I do not know, even now, which is the greater sin: the leaving of that love, or the staying in it for twenty-five years while I died, slowly, one four o'clock morning at a time.

He is a good man. That is the tragedy of it. If he were cruel, it would be easy. But he is good, and kind, and blind, and the goodness and the kindness and the blindness are all tangled up together, and I could not separate them, and so I stopped trying.

I chose the fire. And the fire has cost me, and it will cost me more. But the cold was costing me everything I was, and I was not willing to pay it anymore.

That is the truth. The whole truth. And I am not sorry for it, and I am not proud of it. I am only, at last, honest.

And so we made our peace with the ordinary. Not because it was what we wanted, but because it was the price of what we had, and we had decided, together, that the price was worth paying. The secret would hold, not because we were lucky, but because we would make it hold, with care and with rules and with the long, patient discipline of people who have decided, once and for all, what they are willing to risk.

There would be rules. No messages after dark. No marks above the collar. No photographs in the house. And never, ever, the word that could undo it all, spoken where the walls could hear.

It was not the life I had dreamed of at seventeen, under the neem tree. But it was a life, and it was mine, and it had him in it, and that, in the end, was the only thing the girl under the neem tree had ever wanted.

A road. A hand to hold. A fire that did not go out.

And she had all three now. She had all three, and she was not letting go.

That night, after Ravi had gone to bed and the house had settled, I stood at the window of my room and looked out at the street, and I heard, behind me, the soft sound of a door, and then his footsteps, and then his warmth, and he came and stood behind me, and he put his arms around me, and he rested his chin on my shoulder, and we stood there together, in the dark, looking out at the world that would never know.

"What are you thinking?" he said.

"I am thinking," I said, "about the woman who stood at this window a few months ago, before any of this, and looked out at the same street, and felt nothing. No fire. No fear. No wanting. Just the long, grey flatness of a life that had already been decided for her."

"And now?"

"Now," I said, and I turned in his arms, and I looked up at him, and I saw, in the faint light from the street, the whole of my own fire reflected back at me, "now I feel everything. And I am not afraid of any of it anymore."

He kissed me, then, slow and warm, and the kiss was not the beginning of anything, and not the end of anything. It was the middle, the long, sweet, ordinary middle, the part of the story that nobody writes down because it is too quiet, and too good, and too alive.

And that, I understood, was the real secret. Not that we had found each other. That we had found, in the middle of all the hiding and the danger and the sin, something that could survive the ordinary. A fire that did not need the dark to burn.

A fire that burned, steady and warm, in the light of an ordinary room, on an ordinary night, in an ordinary house, where a husband slept in one room and a son held his mother in another, and the world, outside the window, went on being exactly what it was, and never once, not once, looked in.

And we stood there, and we held each other, and the night went on, and the secret held, and it was, in the end, enough.

It was more than enough.

It was everything.

---

I want to write about my body, once more, before this is over. Because I began this story by telling you about my body, the way a woman tells a secret, and I want to end it the same way, so that you can see, as I have seen, what the fire did to it.

It is the same body. Forty-four years old. The hips still wide, the breasts still heavy, the navel still a small dark well, the rear still large and soft and unashamed. Nothing about it has changed, and everything about it has changed. It carries marks now, small dark blooms at my throat and my hips, that I press my fingers to in the dark, the way a woman presses a letter to her chest. It carries an ache, low and sweet, that returns every time I sit down and remember. And it carries, under the saree and the sindoor and the four o'clock mornings, a heat that no longer frightens me, and no longer shames me, and no longer, thank every god, goes away.

I used to describe this body as a thing that happened to me, a thing I carried and served and hid. And now I know what it is. It is the thing that happened to the world when it tried to make me small, and I refused. It is the thing that bloomed, late and sudden and unstoppable, the way a flower blooms after a fire. It is the body of a woman who has been loved, and has loved, and is no longer afraid of either.

And it is mine. All of it. The marks, the ache, the heat, the softness, the strength. It has always been mine. I have only now, at last, learned how to live in it.

And I want to say this to the woman who began this story, the one who stood in front of her mirror and described her body the way a woman describes a house she has lived in all her life without ever once walking through the front door. I want to tell her that the house was never empty. That the rooms she was afraid to enter were full of things that had been waiting for her, patiently, since before she knew their names. And that the man who would one day walk through the door and light every lamp in the house was already on his way, and had been on his way for a very long time.

She would not have believed me, of course. She was too busy hiding, too busy serving, too busy being the woman the colony expected. And that is all right. That is more than all right. Because every hidden year, every swallowed want, every four o'clock morning, was kindling. And when the fire finally came, it found a house that was ready to burn.

And it burned. God help me, it burned, and I am still burning, and I will be burning for the rest of my life, and the only thing I regret is that I did not light the match myself, years ago, when I first felt the heat.

---

There is one more thing, and I will write it plainly, because I promised myself I would.

This story has been about many things. About a man and his mother, and a secret, and a fall. But it has also been, and I did not see this until the end, about a woman learning, at forty-four, that she was not the sum of what the world had decided she was allowed to be. That the wanting she had buried, the fire she had covered over, was not a weakness to be hidden, but a power to be used.

And I intend to use it. Not only in the dark, in the locked rooms and the stolen hours. Everywhere. In the kitchen, and the temple, and the dance school, and the colony. I intend to walk through the rest of my life the way I walked through that last night: with my eyes open, and my hands free, and my fire, finally, unafraid.

I will not tell you I am going to leave him, or confess, or burn the house down. I am going to do something far more dangerous. I am going to stay. And I am going to grow. The woman who was afraid to want is gone, and in her place is a woman who wants, and who intends to have, and who understands, for the first time, that the wanting and the having are not sins to be confessed, but engines to be stoked.

I do not yet know what shape it will take. A school of my own, perhaps, bigger than the one I teach in now. A name that people say without the click of a tongue. A life that is mine in a way no life has ever been mine. I only know that the fire is lit, and that it is not going out, and that whatever I build with it, I will build with my own two hands, and with him beside me, and with no one's permission but my own.

And that, I think, is what the mad man whose name I have borrowed for this story was always trying to say. Not that the fall is the point. That the rising is. And that a woman, once she has been to the bottom of herself and come back, is no longer afraid of heights.

What do I know now, that I did not know then? I know that a woman can be forty-four years old and still be a girl, still be a stranger to herself, still be waiting for a life that has not begun. And I know that the beginning, when it comes, does not look the way you thought it would. It looks like a locked door, and a blue saree, and a voice on the phone, and a hand under a table, and a rooftop at night, and a room with candles, and the face of the one person who has seen you all along.

I know that the world will call it a sin, and that I will let it, and that the letting will not change a single thing about what I feel. I know that love and sin are not opposites, but neighbours, and that they share a wall, and that the wall, in the end, is only a wall.

And I know that I would do it all again. Every step. Every yes. Every fall. Not because I was right, and not because I was wrong, but because I was, at last, alive, and the being alive was the only thing I had ever really wanted, and I had it now, and I was not going to let it go.

And I know, too, that the next time I stand in front of my dance class, in my school saree, with the ghungroos on my ankles and the stick in my hand, I will not look away from my own reflection. I will look at the woman in the mirror, the teacher, the mother, the wife, the woman who has been loved into life, and I will see her, and I will let the girls see her too, not the performance, but the woman under it, steady and warm and unafraid.

And I will tap the floor, and I will count the beat, and I will teach them the only thing I have ever truly learned to teach: that a woman who knows her own worth does not need to be told where to stand.

She takes the center of the floor, and she dances.

I do not know what the future holds. I do not know if the secret will hold, or if the world will find us out, or what the morning after the last morning will bring. I know only that I am no longer waiting for permission. I stopped waiting, somewhere between the blue saree and the rooftop, between the first yes and the last.

I began this story, or the mad man who told it through me began it, by speaking of a bottomless pit of myriad sensations, and I did not understand then what those words meant. I understand now. They were not a warning, and they were not a threat. They were a map. The pit was me. It was always me. The sensations were the wanting, and the fire, and the fall, and the rising, and the love, and the fear, and the thousand other things I had spent my whole life holding at arm's length.

And the bottom, the bottom I was so afraid of, was not a fall at all. It was a landing. It was the place where I finally stopped running, and turned around, and found that everything I had been running from was already there, waiting, with its arms open.

And I fell, and I landed, and I am still landing, and I will be landing for the rest of my life.

And it is the most alive I have ever been.

I am Priya. And I am finally, ruinously, unashamed.

And if you ask the women in my colony to describe me, they will still lower their voices, and click their tongues, and call me sanskari.

And they will be right.

And they will be wrong.

And I will not care.

---

*End of Part Twenty.*

*The end.*
