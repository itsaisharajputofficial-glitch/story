A Mad Man’s Fiction

Part Nine

This is a work of fiction of a mad man with mad tastes.

The day after the temple, I tried to be a wife again.

I do not know why I tried. Perhaps because the morning light made everything I had done look different, the way the kitchen looks different at four in the morning and again at noon. Perhaps because Ravi came down for his tea and squeezed my shoulder, once, the way he used to twenty years ago, and said, “You are looking well, Priya. The temple does you good.” And the kindness of it, the blind, ordinary kindness of it, landed in my chest like a stone.

If he knew, I thought, for the thousandth time. If he knew what his wife did in the shadow of that temple while the bell rang. If he knew what his son’s hand was doing while he slept in the next room.

But he did not know. That was the arrangement now. He did not know, and I did not tell, and the whole house ran on the silence between those two facts, the way a train runs on rails you cannot see.

So I tried to be a wife. I made the breakfast. I folded his clothes. I did not look at my phone. And all day, under the trying, the hunger moved, patient and certain, the way it had moved all week, and I understood that trying was not the same as being, and that I was no longer a wife.

I was something else. Something with a name I could not say in daylight.

And yet the day would not let me pretend for long. Every small thing betrayed me. The smell of the jasmine I pinned in my hair took me back to the temple corridor and the taste of camphor. The cool water from the tap on my wrists took me back to the phone in the dark, to his voice saying come for me, maa. The sight of Ravi’s reading glasses left on the arm of the sofa took me back to the dinner that had not happened yet, the dinner I was already dreading and already, God help me, already wanting.

I was living two days at once. The day I was moving through, all sunlight and chores and ordinary duty, and the day that was waiting for me at the end of it, all green silk and low light and a hand I could already feel on my thigh. And the second day was the real one. I knew that now. The ordinary day was only the skin over it, the way a saree is the skin over a woman’s body, hiding everything and promising it at the same time.

By four o’clock I had stopped pretending. I went upstairs, and I bathed, and I opened the cupboard, and I took out the green saree, and I did not once ask myself what I was doing.

By evening, the house had settled into its ordinary shape, and the dinner sat before me like a verdict.

I had worn the green saree. The one he had asked for. The one that showed the soft curve of my waist when I reached across the table. I had worn it without argument, without even a flicker of hesitation, and that was the thing that frightened me most: not the saree, but the ease. The way my hands had picked it out of the cupboard the way they once picked out a temple saree. The way obedience had begun to feel less like surrender and more like breathing.

Ten minutes before dinner, as I stood at the bedroom mirror doing my hair, he came up behind me.

I saw him in the glass first, a shadow in the doorway, and then his reflection crossed the room, and his hands closed on my shoulders, and I went very still. He was in his home clothes, a clean shirt, his hair still damp from washing, and he smelled of soap and something sharp underneath, and his eyes, meeting mine in the mirror, were already the wolf’s.

“You will sit beside me tonight,” he said, and his hands slid down my arms, slow, and came to rest on my waist. “And you will wear nothing under this saree.”

“Deva,” I breathed, “your father,”

He released me then, and he stepped back, and he said, in that low, unhurried voice, “No petticoat. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Deva,” I whispered, and I watched my own face in the mirror as I said it, and the woman in the glass did not look horrified. She looked ready.

“Good girl. Five minutes.”

And he was gone, and I stood at the mirror and I finished my hair, and I did not put on the petticoat.

Kamala had laid the table. The rotis were stacked under a cloth. The dal was in its steel bowl, the sabzi in its kadai, the rice steaming. Ravi sat at the head of the table, his glasses on, already reaching for the water jug, and Deva sat to his right, and there was an empty chair beside Deva, and I knew, the way a deer knows the sound of a branch snapping, that I was meant to take it.

“Sit, maa,” Deva said, without looking at me. “The food will get cold.”

Innocent. To anyone listening, the most innocent thing in the world. And to me, the words were a hand already reaching under the table.

I sat beside him.

The dinner began the way every dinner of my married life had begun.

Ravi talked. He talked about the shop, about the price of oil, about the new boy he had hired who could not add two numbers, about his knee, about the temple committee meeting on Thursday. He talked, and I served, and Deva ate, and the three of us made the small, familiar sounds of a family at its table, and if you had watched us from the doorway, you would have seen nothing at all. You would have seen a mother, a father, a grown son, and a quiet, well-cooked meal.

You would have been wrong.

Because under the table, hidden by the long fall of the green saree, his hand had found my knee.

It was the lightest touch. His palm, warm through the silk, settling on my knee the way a man settles his hand on his own armrest. And then, very slowly, it began to move.

I did not stop it. I did not shift away. I sat there with my back straight and my face calm and I passed Ravi the salt when he asked for it, and all the while his hand slid up, over the silk, over my thigh, and the heat of it spread through me like a fever, and I kept my eyes on my plate and my voice steady, and the whole world narrowed to the slow, terrible, wonderful journey of that hand.

“The food is very good today, maa,” Deva said, and his voice was the voice of a son complimenting his mother’s cooking, and his fingers were drawing slow circles on the inside of my thigh.

“Thank you, beta,” I said, and my voice did not shake. “Eat properly. You have grown thin.”

“I will.” His hand moved higher. “I have been hungry all week.”

Ravi grunted, reaching for another roti. “The boy eats like a bird and says he is hungry. Priya, give him more dal. He looks pale.”

I reached for the dal, and the movement brought my body closer to his hand, and his fingers curled, just slightly, into the soft flesh of my thigh, and I had to bite the inside of my cheek to keep the sound in my throat where it belonged.

“Here, beta,” I said, and I ladled the dal into his bowl, and my hand was steady, and my heart was a drum.

“The food is very hot today,” he said, and he looked at me then, and his eyes, over the rim of his water glass, were black and burning and full of a meaning that Ravi, lifting a roti, would never in a thousand years have seen. “I like it hot, maa.”

“Then eat slowly,” I said, and I heard my own voice as if from a great distance, calm and warm and motherly, and under the table his hand had reached the top of my thigh, and then, and I felt my whole body go rigid, it slid inward, and it found bare skin.

I had not worn the petticoat.

I had not even considered it. Some part of me, the part that had stopped arguing with itself, had known from the moment I took the green saree off its hanger exactly what this dinner was going to be, and had dressed me accordingly. The saree was the only cloth between his hand and the burning center of me, and he knew it now, I saw it in the way his jaw tightened, the way his fingers stilled for one long second, and then moved, deliberately, into the heat.

Ravi looked up from his plate. “The dal is good. Not too hot. You are both imagining things.” He reached for the pickle. “Kamala!”

From the kitchen, Kamala’s voice: “Coming, saab.”

I had one second. One second in which my husband called for the maid and my son’s fingers were an inch from the ruin of me, and I did the only thing I could do. I crossed my ankles, tight, trapping his hand between my thighs, and I turned to Ravi, and I smiled, and I said, “She is busy with the dishes. What do you need, I will get it.”

“The pickle,” Ravi said, and went back to his roti, and did not see, did not see anything.

Under the table, his hand did not move. It did not need to. It was exactly where it wanted to be, pressed against the wet heat of me, held there by my own thighs, and the humiliation of it, and the thrill of it, were so tangled together that I could not have pulled them apart with a knife.

Kamala came in anyway, with the pickle, and set it down, and her eyes went around the table, quick and sharp, and I felt, for one cold, terrible second, that she could see through the cloth and the wood and the twenty-five years of pretense, straight down to the hand that was pressed between my thighs under the green saree.

“Anything else, madam?” she said.

“No, Kamala,” I said, and my voice was a marvel of composure. “You can go. I will clear the table.”

She looked at me for a beat too long, and then she nodded and went back to the kitchen, and I understood, with a clarity that turned my stomach to ice and my cunt to fire at the same time, that the maid knew something. Not everything. But something. And that knowledge, that extra pair of eyes in the house, only made the hand under the table burn hotter.

Because danger, I was learning, was not the thing I had to endure. Danger was the thing that made it sweet.

Halfway through the meal, Ravi pushed his plate away and said, “Priya, bring me some water. The jug is empty.”

The words hit me like a bucket of cold water. I would have to stand. His hand was between my thighs, buried in the wet heat of me, and to stand I would have to close my legs, to rise, to move, and every one of those small acts was an impossible, public confession.

I opened my mouth, and no sound came, and then, before the silence could stretch into something Ravi might notice, Deva spoke, smooth and easy, “I will get it, papa. You have been on your feet all day, maa. Sit.”

And he stood, and his hand slipped out of me, and I felt its absence like a wound, a sudden cold where the heat had been, and I did not look at him, I looked at Ravi, who was already saying something about the new boy at the shop, and I smiled, and I nodded, and I was a wife whose son was fetching water, nothing more.

When he came back, he set the jug down, and he sat, and under the table his hand returned to my thigh, and it was cold now, cold with the condensation from the jug, and when those cold fingers touched my burning skin I had to dig my nails into my palm to keep from crying out, and the sound of the jug hitting the table covered the small, strangled breath that escaped me.

“Thank you, beta,” Ravi said, and drank.

“Anything for the family,” Deva said, and under the table his cold fingers found me again, and I bit the inside of my cheek until I tasted copper.

His fingers moved.

Slow. So slow. One finger, tracing the seam of me through the silk, and then the silk was pushed aside, and there was nothing between his skin and mine, and I felt the first real touch, and the sound that tried to leave my throat was a sound that would have ended everything, and I swallowed it whole, and I reached for my water glass, and I drank, and I did not make a sound.

“Ravi,” I said, when I could trust my voice, “there is the wedding next week. The Sharma girl. We are going, yes?”

It was a lifeline. A normal question. A wife’s question. I threw it into the conversation the way a drowning woman throws a rope, and I did not care where it landed, only that it gave my mouth something to do that was not screaming.

Ravi nodded, chewing. “Saturday. The baraat starts at seven. We should reach by six thirty.” He looked at Deva. “You are coming too, beta. No excuses. The family asked for you specially.”

“Specially?” Deva’s voice was smooth as oil, and under the table his finger was inside me now, one slow, curling inch, and I gripped the edge of the table and prayed. “Why specially?”

Ravi laughed, that short bark of a laugh he kept for weddings. “Because you are the unmarried one, beta. Every aunty with an unmarried daughter will be measuring you for a groom’s suit.” He reached for the water. “There is talk. The Roopa girl. They are bringing her photograph.”

The word landed like a stone in the still pool of my body. Roopa. A girl. A photograph. A marriage. And all at once, under the table, his finger inside me, his thumb on the place that was already throbbing, I felt something rise up in me that had nothing to do with fear and nothing to do with heat.

It was a feeling I did not yet have a name for, and it burned worse than any chili.

But I did not have time to name it. Because at that moment, his thumb pressed down, and his finger curled, and the wave that had been building all week, all day, all through this terrible, wonderful dinner, finally broke.

And I came, silently, at my own dining table, in my green saree, with my husband chewing roti two feet away and my son’s hand between my thighs and the word Roopa still hanging in the air like a bell that would not stop ringing.

“The food was very good, maa,” Deva said, and he reached for his water glass, and I saw, out of the corner of my eye, the same hand that had just been inside me lift the glass and drink, as if it had done nothing at all, as if it were any hand, as if the world had not just ended and begun again.

Ravi pushed back his chair. “I am going up. The knee is bad today.” He stood, and he looked at me, and he said, in that same blind, kind voice that would one day be my undoing, “You have been very happy lately, Priya. Whatever it is, keep doing it. You look ten years younger.”

And he walked out, and the room went very still, and I sat there with the wreckage of myself still cooling under the green saree and my husband’s blessing still hanging in the air, and I thought I might scream, or laugh, or weep, and I did none of them.

“He is right,” Deva said quietly, and he was not looking at me, he was looking at the doorway where his father had gone. “You do look younger. It suits you.” A pause. “Being mine suits you.”

“Deva.”

“Roopa,” he said, and the name came out of his mouth like a coin dropped on the table, bright and hard. “Did it burn, maa? Hearing that name while my hand was inside you?”

I did not answer. I could not. Because the truth of it, the ugly, shocking truth of it, was that it had burned, and he knew it had burned, and the knowing of it was written all over his face when he finally turned to look at me.

“Good,” he said, and he smiled, that slow, dark, wolf smile, and it was the most terrifying and the most beautiful thing I had ever seen. “I wanted to see it. The first spark.” He stood, and he leaned down, and his mouth was at my ear, and he said, “At the wedding on Saturday, you will see girls younger than you, prettier than you, throwing themselves at me. And you will smile, and you will be my mother, and you will die a little with every one of them.” His lips brushed my ear. “And then, when it is over, you will come home and you will show me exactly how much you wanted me to belong to you and no one else.”

And he was gone, and I sat alone at the dining table with the dishes of a normal family dinner cooling around me, and I understood, with a cold, thrilling certainty, that the feeling I could not name, the one that had risen up when Ravi said the word Roopa, was not going to stay unnamed for long.

It had a name. It had always had a name. I had simply never let myself feel it before.

It was jealousy. And it was already eating me alive.
